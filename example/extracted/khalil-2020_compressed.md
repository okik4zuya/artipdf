The effect of Au morphology and its plasmonic vibration mode on the photoactivity of Au–TiO2 nanostructures 
was  evaluated.  Here,  Au  nanoparticles  with  three  distinct  morphologies,  i.e.,  nanooctahedra,  nanocube,  and 
nanorod, were successfully prepared  and integrated into TiO2  nanoparticles. The nanocatalysts were used for 
photocatalytic conversion of bicarbonate to formic acid for solar fuel generation. Based on the result, it is found 
that the integration of Au and TiO2  resulted in a synergistic effect on the production of formic acid due to the 
presence of Au’s SPR phenomenon. Results also demonstrated that the shape of Au nanoparticles plays a pivotal 
role in the improvement of Au–TiO2 activity. From the photocatalytic experiment, Au NR-TiO2 was found to be 
the most active nanocatalyst with a formic acid yield of 5.14 mmol/g cat. By using spectroscopy techniques and 
near-field enhancement simulation, it is believed that the photocatalytic enhancement was due to non-radiative 
(hot  electron  transfer  and  storage)  and  radiative  (near-field  enhancement  and  far-field  scattering)  effects. 
However,  the  additional  shape-dependent  variation  on  activity  was  most  likely  due  to  the  radiative  effects 
instead of non-radiative ones.

1. Introduction 

The depletion of fossil fuels and their negative environmental impact 
has driven researchers to seek alternative renewable energy resources. 
One of the leading technological breakthroughs for solving this issue is 
the utilization of carbon dioxide (CO2) in solar fuel generation [1–3]. 
Among different types of solar fuels, photoconversion of CO2 to formic 
acid has recently attracted much attention as it can serve as precursors in 
the synthesis of many chemicals, hydrogen storage materials, reducing 
agents, and as the reactant in formic acid-based fuel cell [4]. However, 
the large-scale operation is still limited by several challenges due to the 
high  thermodynamic  stability  of  CO2,  making  its  photoreduction  is  a 
difficult  and  energetically  unfavorable process  [5–9]. Besides, several 
practical drawbacks can also be originated from the low CO2 solubility

in water (0.033 M at T: 25 
C and P: 1 atm), which results in the lack of 
productivity and selectivity for formic acid [10]. Nevertheless, this issue 
can be avoided by using bicarbonate ion as the reactant as an alternative 
to the utilization of CO2. Typically, CO2  can easily be captured in the 
form of NaHCO3 using various chemisorption materials such as sodium 
metasilicate (Na2SiO3) or encapsulate liquid sorbent [11,12]. A recent 
study by Leonard and co-workers have suggested that photoreduction of 
bicarbonate was found to be more favorable than that of CO2 [13]. It is 
reported that bicarbonate is more favorable to form coordination with 
active  catalytic centers on the surface of TiO2, and  thus facilitating a 
more straightforward reduction [14,15]. Therefore, the development of 
suitable  catalytic  materials  for  the  photoreduction  of  bicarbonate  is 
desired. 

Currently, TiO2  has been widely used as a catalyst in the photore-
duction  of  bicarbonate  due  to  its  excellent  chemical  stability,  cost- 
effectiveness  and  environmental  friendliness  [5,16].  The  mechanism 
of TiO2  in facilitating the reaction is based on the photogeneration of 
charge carriers, i.e., electrons and holes, upon the absorption of photon 
energy from the light source [17]. Typically, the photogenerated elec-
trons may be used to reduce the available bicarbonate ions, while the 
holes  are  scavenged  by  the  sacrificing  donors  [18,19].  However,  the 
application  of  TiO2  for  large-scale  solar  fuel  operation  remains  chal-
lenging, partly because of the mismatch between the solar light energy 
(mainly at visible region) and the excitation energy of TiO2  (bandgap 
energy, Eg: ~3.2 eV) that lies in the UV region [20,21]. In addition, fast 
recombination of photogenerated electrons and holes was also reported 
to be responsible for further reduction in photocatalytic efficiency. 

Over the last several years, various strategies have been employed to 
enhance the catalytic activity of TiO2 and Recently to avoid the occur-
rence of the previously mentioned shortcomings. For instance, different 
types  of  photosensitizers, such  as organic  dyes, transition metal com-
plexes, or small bandgap semiconductors such as quantum dots, have 
been used to widen the light absorption spectrum of TiO2 to match solar 
radiation  [22–24].  It  was  reported  that,  upon  solar  irradiation,  these 
photosensitizers  could  inject  their  photogenerated  electrons  to  the 
conduction band (CB) of TiO2, resulting in an increase in the population 
of hot electrons essential for CO2  conversion. In other studies, integra-
tion with plasmonic particles, like Au or Ag nanoparticles, could also be 
used  to  enhance  TiO2  photocatalytic  activity  [25,26].  Literature  sug-
gested that surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of plasmonic particles was 
able to not only broaden the light absorption ability to visible light re-
gion  but  also  to  prevent  fast  charges  recombination  [27,28].  In  our 
previous study, we reported that the plasmon-induced enhancement of 
TiO2  photocatalytic  activity  was  primarily  due  to  several  synergistic 
radiative and non-radiative effects, i.e. (1) SPR-generated hot electrons 
migration  to  TiO2’s  CB,  (2)  generation  of  intense  local  electric  field 
“hot-spot” near TiO2-plasmonic particles interface, and (3) the ability of 
plasmonic particles to act as the storage for photogenerated electrons, 
and thus preventing fast charges recombination [29]. Nevertheless, very 
few studies have been conducted to investigate the effect of plasmonic 
vibration on the photocatalytic enhancement of TiO2. In many reports, it 
has  been  widely  reported  that  the  vibration  modes  of  plasmonic 

nanoparticles, especially Au, were significantly affected by the size and 
morphology of the particles [30,31]. 

In  this  study,  the  role  of  Au  nanoparticle’s  shape  to  the  photo-
catalytic activity of nano Au–TiO2  heterostructures was studied. Here, 
the effect of plasmonic vibration modes was evaluated by fabricating the 
nano-heterostructures,  where  Au  nanoparticles  were  made  in  three 
different morphological features (nanooctahedra, nanocube, and nano-
rod). In this work, the as-prepared nano-heterostructures were used as 
catalysts  for  the  photoreduction  of  bicarbonate  to  formic  acid. 
Furthermore, the proposed catalytic mechanism was also studied using 
both  experimental  and  theoretical  approaches  to  provide  comprehen-
sive information in the mechanism of as-prepared nanocatalyst that fa-
cilitates the reduction of bicarbonate to formic acid. 

2. Experimental section 

2.1. Materials 

Gold (III) chloride hydrate (HAuCl4.xH2O) (purity: >99.5%), sodium 
citrate  (purity:  >99%),  L-ascorbic  acid  (purity:  >99%),  sodium  boro-
hydride  (NaBH4)  (purity:  >98%),  hexadecyltrimethylammonium  bro-
mide (CTAB) (purity: > 98%) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) (purity: >99%) 
were  purchased  from  Sigma-Aldrich  and  used  for  the  synthesis  of  Au 
nanoparticle with different morphological features. For the synthesis of 
anatase TiO2  nanoparticles, titanium (IV) butoxide (Ti(OBu)4) (purity: 
>97%), NH4OH solution (28–30% NH3  in H2O) and absolute ethanol 
were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used  without further purifica-
tion.  Additionally,  hydrochloric  acid  (HCl)  were  also  purchased  from 
Sigma-Aldrich and used in the fabrication of the nanocatalysts. Finally, 
sodium  bicarbonate  (NaHCO3),  methanol,  formic  acid  (HCOOH)  and 
potassium  dihydrogen  phosphate  (KH2PO4)  were  purchased  from 
Sigma-Aldrich and used in photocatalytic experiments. 

2.2. Synthesis of Au nanooctahedra (Au NO) 

In this work, Au NO was prepared via hydrothermal method [32]. 
Here, monodisperse Au nanooctahedra was synthesized by making an 
aqueous solution containing 0.055 g of CTAB, 50 μL of 0.1 M sodium 
citrate, 250 μL of 0.01 M HAuCl4.xH2O and 9.7 mL of deionized water. 
The mixture was then heated at 110 
C for 6 h for the reaction to take 
place. After the reaction, the mixture was then cooled to room temper-
ature and centrifugated at 6000 rpm for 20 min to get rid of excess CTAB 
residue. The precipitate was then collected and re-dispersed in deionized 
water for further characterizations.

2.8. Photocatalytic experiments 

To study the influence of plasmonic vibration modes on the photo-
catalytic activity of Au–TiO2 nanocatalysts, photocatalytic reduction of 
bicarbonate  to  formic  acid  was  carried  out.  25  mL  of  buffer  solution 
containing 0.3 M NaHCO3  and 2 M of glycerol as sacrificing donor in 
deionized water were prepared as the initial reaction mixture. Subse-
quently, 0.25 mg of each nanocatalyst with different Au morphologies, i. 
e., nanooctahedra, nanocube, and nanorod, were added into the reaction 
mixture and stirred in the dark to achieve adsorption equilibrium. Af-
terward, the photocatalytic reaction was done in a quartz sealed tube 
and placed under solar simulator with irradiation of light source (150 W 
Xenon arc lamp, Zolix LSP-X150, China) for 40 min. Here, the horizontal 
light intensity was approximately 320 cd. An aliquot of the samples was 
collected at every 10 min increments, and the concentration of formic 
acid  was  determined  using  high-performance  liquid  chromatography 
(HPLC). The analyses were performed using Agilent 1200 HPLC system 
with  ZORBAX  SB  RRHT  C18  column  and  UV  detector.  For  the  deter-
mination of  formic acid,  a  mixture  of 0.1%  of  KH2PO4  and  methanol 
(95:5) was used as the mobile phase, and the detection was conducted at 
λ ¼ 220 nm. Furthermore, quantification of formic acid concentration 
was done by comparing the peak area of the sample with a calibration 
curve  made  from  standard  formic  acid  solutions  (see  Supporting 
Information).

3. Results and discussions 

3.1. Preparation and characterization of the nanocatalysts 

Fig. 1a presents the XRD pattern of the reaction products obtained 
from the sol-gel reaction. Based on the result, it is evident that the high- 
temperature  calcination  process  was  responsible  for  the  formation  of

was also reported elsewhere [39,40]. The diffractogram revealed that all 
the  Bragg’s  peaks  for  the  calcined  sol-gel  reaction  products  could

surface  area  analysis  was  also  revealed  that  the  resulting  TiO2  nano-

An additional evidence for the formation as well as the morphology 
of the nanocatalysts was also obtained from TEM analyses. Fig. 3 shows 
the micrographic images of the as-prepared nanoparticles and the cor-
responding nanocatalysts. Based on the result, it is apparent that the sol- 
gel reaction followed by high-temperature calcination could be used to 
fabricate uniform spherical TiO2 nanoparticles with an average particle 
size of ~300 nm (Fig. 3a). The formation of the anatase crystal phase 
was  further  proven  from  the  high-resolution  imaging  (Fig.  3e).  Ac-
cording to result, it is evident that the as-prepared TiO2  nanoparticles 
were well faceted with two observable lattice fringes at 0.35 and 0.24 
nm,  which  could  be  discerned  as  (101)  and  (103)  lattice  planes  for 
anatase, respectively [5,23,25,29,41,43]. This is also supported by the 
result obtained from the FFT analyses (Fig. 3e, inset). 

Furthermore, low magnification TEM observations also revealed that 
monodisperse Au nanoparticles with octahedral, cubic, and rod shapes 
were successfully synthesized (Fig. 3b–d). As shown, both Au NO and Au 
NC exhibited narrow particle size distribution with an average particle 
size of about ~50 nm and ~10 nm, respectively. The formation of such 
well-structured  Au morphologies was believed due to the presence of 
CTAB and sodium citrate as the crystal growth directing agents [32,33]. 
Investigations  with  high-resolution  imaging  and  FFT  analyses  also 
showed that both Au nanoparticles exhibited the (111) Au face-centered 
cubic  (FCC)  lattice  planes,  which  were  observed  at  0.24  nm  lattice 
fringes (Fig. 3f and g) [31,59]. On the other hand, results also demon-
strated that the as-prepared Au NR had an aspect ratio average of ~5 
with diameter and length of the rod particles of around 12 and 59 nm, 
respectively  (Fig.  3d).  The  formation  of  well-faceted  Au  NR  was  also 
supported by the appearance of Au (111) FCC planes in both HR-TEM 
images  and  FFT  analysis  (Fig.  3h).  According  to  Nikoobakht  and 
El-Sayed,  the  formation  of  such  unique  and  monodisperse  rod-like 
morphology was believed due to the presence of crystal facet directing 
agents, i.e., AgNO3 and CTAB, during Au crystal growth reaction [34]. It 
is suggested that AgNO3 and CTAB could react to form AgBr, which has 
the  ability  to  induce  the  formation  of  rod-like  particle  structures.  A 
detailed crystal growth mechanism for the seed-mediated synthesis of 
Au NR in the presence of AgNO3  and CTAB has also been comprehen-
sively reviewed elsewhere [60]. 

Results from TEM images also demonstrated that the as-prepared Au 
nanoparticles  with  different  morphological  features  were  successfully 
incorporated  with  TiO2  to  form  the  corresponding  Au–TiO2  nano-
catalysts  (Fig.  3i–k).  Based  on  the  result,  the  integration  of  the  two 
materials could easily be identified by the appearance of several small 
Au  nanoparticles  with  distinct  particle  shapes  on  the  surface  of  large 
sphere-like TiO2 nanoparticles. Besides, under the electron microscope, 
Au nanoparticles could easily be differentiated as they typically have a 
darker contrast than TiO2 due to their high electron density [61].

carried  out  to  evaluate  the  photocatalytic  activity  of  the  as-prepared 
nanocatalysts. In this work, the performance of each nanocatalyst was 
assessed by monitoring the resulting formic acid concentration during 
light irradiation. Fig. 4 shows the performance of the as-prepared pris-

photocatalytic  activity  of  TiO2  nanoparticles  could  be  enhanced  by 
integrating Au nanoparticles. Under artificial solar light irradiation at 
ambient  reaction  condition,  the  production  of  formic  acid  using 
different types of Au–TiO2 nanocatalysts were at least two times greater 
than the pristine TiO2 nanoparticles. According to recent studies by Pan 
and  co-workers,  the  observed  photocatalytic  enhancement  could  be 
attributed due to the synergistic contribution of Au’s SPR effect [18,19]. 
Similar  photocatalytic  improvement  was  also  obtained  when  such 
nanostructures were used in other reactions such as photodegradation of 
organic dyes/pollutants [23,25,26,29,61]. 

It  is  argued  that  Au  nanoparticles  can  readily  absorb  visible  light 
energy due to the SPR effect. The photoexcited plasmons can then non- 
radiatively decay to create an impermanent non-equilibrium electron- 
hole  pair,  where  the  hot  electrons  can  be  transferred  to  the  adjacent 
TiO2’s CB via quantum tunneling, provided the energy of the hot elec-
trons is larger than the Schottky barrier (SB) height (ΦSB) at the interface 
between the metal and semiconductor [62–64]. Typically, the energy of 
these hot electrons is in range of 1–4 eV for Au nanoparticles, while the 
value of ΦSB in Au–TiO2 interface was reported to be around 0.15–0.23 
eV  [65–67].  The  evidence  for  such  migrations  has  also  been  experi-
mentally  investigated  using  IR  probe  and  femtosecond  transient  ab-
sorption spectroscopy [68,69]. Additionally, this phenomenon can also 
be noticed through the shift of VB of Au–TiO2  with respect to the cor-
responding  pristine  TiO2  nanoparticles  [70].  It  is  reported  that  both 
electron transfer and the formation of SB can drive the Fermi level into 
more negative potential causing the position of TiO2’s VB to shift to the 
lower binding energy. In this work, the calculated Au–TiO2’s VB posi-
tions were found to be less positive than the pristine TiO2’s VB (Table 1). 
Nevertheless, since spectral light absorption overlap between TiO2 and 
Au’s  SPR  was  absent  (Fig.  1b),  the  plasmon-induced  energy  transfer 
cannot  be  the  main  reason  for  the  observed  photocatalytic  enhance-
ment.  Besides,  the  photocatalytic  enhancement  due  to  the  plasmonic 
heating under solar irradiation was also irrelevant, considering the high 
thermal energy required for CO2 to formic acid conversion (0.61 V) [5, 
71].

also be affected by the polarizability (the ability to polarize the incoming 
light) of the particle, which typically dictated by its geometry. In many 
cases, this polarization is often observed in non-spherical structures with 
sharp edges such as nanocube and nanooctahedra [76–78]. 

Furthermore, a similar phenomenon was also observed in near-field 
enhancement.  According  to  literature,  the  presence  of  SPR  from  Au 
nanoparticles could induce the formation of intense local electromag-
netic field near the surface of Au nanoparticles [79]. This localized field 
enhancement can induce the formation of “hot-spot” regions, where the 
rate of charge carriers generation is 1000 times larger than that of the 
incident electromagnetic field [70]. Therefore, the population of pho-
togenerated  electrons  and  holes  is  expected  to  be  higher  within  this 
region. Based on the result, it is clear that the near-field enhancement 
was found to be affected by the type of vibrational modes. In the lon-
gitudinal  mode,  the  result  demonstrated  the  magnitude  of  field 
enhancement  was  not  affected  by  the  variation  of  Au  morphology 
(Fig.  5-e).  However,  the  formation  of  the  “hot-spot”  region  due  to 
near-field enhancement became more pronounced when transverse vi-
bration  mode  was  applied  (Fig.  5f–h).  As  a  result,  the  photocatalytic 
activity of Au–TiO2  nanocatalyst was also expected to be higher since 
the population of hot electrons and holes essential for the photocatalytic 
reaction was increased. It is also worth to note that the magnitude of 
near-field  enhancement  was  more  prominent  in  Au  NR-TiO2  and  Au 
NO–TiO2 due to the better polarizability of Au nanoparticles.

4. Conclusion 

The  performance  of  the  as-prepared  Au–TiO2  nanocatalysts  for  an 
enhanced visible-light photoreduction of bicarbonate to formic acid has 
been successfully investigated in this study. The influence of plasmonic 
vibration  modes  on  the  overall  catalytic  activity  was  evaluated  by 
fabricating  the  nanocatalysts  with  different  Au  morphologies,  i.e., 
nanooctahedra, nanocube, and nanorod. Based on the result, the inte-
gration of Au nanoparticles to TiO2 was found to be responsible for the 
substantial  improvement in the production of formic acid, due to the 
presence  of  synergistic  effects  of  Au’s  SPR  phenomenon  via  non- 
radiative  (hot  electron  transfer  and  storage)  and  radiative  (near-field 

8

M. Khalil et al.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  

Materials Chemistry and Physics 249 (2020) 123018

enhancement and far-field scattering) effects. Besides, the shape of Au 
nanoparticles plays a critical role in the improvement of Au–TiO2 pho-
tocatalytic activity. Au NR-TiO2 was able to facilitate the production of 
formic acid by nearly five times the amount produced by pristine TiO2. 
Meanwhile,  the  yield  of  formic  acid  was  only  increased  by  ~3  times 
when  Au  NO–TiO2  and  Au  NC-TiO2  were  used  as  the  catalysts.  It  is 
believed  that  the  observed  shape-dependent  enhancement  in  the 
photoreduction of bicarbonate was mainly contributed by the radiative 
effects rather than the non-radiative one. Nevertheless, the contribution 
from the non-radiative effect due to the plasmon energy transfer from 
the additional longitudinal band in Au NR-TiO2 shall not be neglected.
