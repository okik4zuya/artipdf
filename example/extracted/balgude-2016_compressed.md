Nanostructured layered Sn3O4 for hydrogen
production and dye degradation under sunlight

S. D. Balgude,a Y. A. Sethi,a B. B. Kale,*a N. R. Munirathnam,a D. P. Amalnerkarb
and P. V. Adhyapak*a

Nanostructured layered Sn3O4 nanoplatelets were synthesized via a facile hydrothermal method. At low pH,
the formation of SnO2 was observed due to the existance of Sn(II). By tuning the pH of the hydrothermal
reaction, Sn3O4 was conferred. The broadening of XRD peaks reveals the existance of nanocrystalline
triclinic Sn3O4, which was also conﬁrmed by Raman spectroscopy. Morphological examination displays
the nanostructured layered plate-like structure with diameter of 20 nm and length of 100–150 nm. The
optical band gap was observed to be 2.76 eV for Sn3O4. Considering the band gap was in the visible light
region,
investigations on the photocatalytic production of hydrogen and dye degradation were
performed under sunlight. A maximum hydrogen evolution (3916 mmol h(cid:1)1/0.5 g) was obtained using the
Sn3O4 nanoparticles, which is much higher than that found with SnO2 (729 mmol h(cid:1)1/0.5 g) due to
narrowing of the band gap. Hydrogen production from H2S using Sn3O4 was reported for the ﬁrst time.
Sn3O4 also demonstrates excellent dye degradation activity. The high photocatalytic activity of Sn3O4
was attributed to its nanocrystalline nature and narrowing of the band gap.

Various semiconducting materials such as TiO2, ZnO, Fe2O3,
SnO2, CdS and ZnS have been customarily used as photo-
catalysts that act as sensitizers for light induced redox-
processes. Among such metal oxide semiconductor mate-
rials, tin oxides with diﬀerent forms viz. SnO, SnO2 and
Sn3O4, possess a special place owing to their wide-spread
applications in catalysis, sensors, transparent electrodes,
lithium-ion batteries, solar cells etc.1–4 Their abundant
availability and non-toxicity have made tin oxides popular
contenders. Stannous (SnO) and stannic (SnO2) are two
univalent forms of tin oxide with the Sn2+ and Sn4+ oxidation
states. Both compounds exhibit quite diﬀerent physico-
chemical characteristics and have diﬀerent applications.5–7
Similarly, heterovalent tin oxides such as Sn5O6, Sn3O4 and
Sn2O3 have also been reported by some authors both theo-
retically8,9 and experimentally.10,11 However, these hetero-
valent oxide materials have not yet been fully explored due to
the diﬃculties associated with obtaining stable and pure
crystalline phases when compared with the stoichiometric
monovalent oxides. Sn3O4 was reported as the rst identied
non-stoichiometric tin oxide and has been investigated in
terms of its crystal structure later.10 The Sn3O4 band structure

reports on the synthesis of high quality pure Sn3O4 using

catalyst. C. J. Damaschio et al. have reported the growth of
Sn3O4 nanobelt using a carbothermal reduction process.15 W.
Xu et al. have synthesized hierarchical Sn3O4 nanoowers

Sn3O4.19 P. H. Suman et al. have studied the gas sensing

have enlightened the scope to study such materials in view of
their photocatalytic ability. In the context of Sn3O4 as a pho-
tocatalyst, very limited studies have been reported. In view of
this, we have focused on the synthesis of Sn3O4 and its
photocatalytic activity.

We report the simple hydrothermal synthesis of nano-
structured layered Sn3O4. The as-synthesized samples were
characterized using XRD, FE-SEM, FE-TEM, Raman, PL and UV-
visible spectroscopy. The photocatalytic activities of the as-
synthesized samples were evaluated by hydrogen evolution via
H2S splitting under sunlight irradiation as well as the degra-
dation of methylene blue (MB) dye. To the best of our knowl-
edge, this is the rst report on hydrogen generation via H2S
splitting using a Sn3O4 photocatalyst.

2 Experimental
Materials

All reagents used were of AR grade and used as received without
further purication. Stannous chloride dihydrate (SnCl2$2H2O)
and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Merck
(India). Methylene Blue (MB) was purchased from SD Fine
Chemical Co. Double distilled water was used as the solvent for
the synthesis.

Synthesis of Sn3O4 and SnO2 nanoparticles
For the preparation of Sn3O4, 7 g of SnCl2$2H2O was dissolved
in 120 mL of water using a magnetic stirrer at 500 rpm. A 0.1 M
solution of NaOH was added dropwise to this solution till the
pH of the solution becomes highly alkaline (pH 13). The reac-
tion mixture appears as a milky white colloidal suspension at
this stage. The resultant mixture was magnetically stirred for
30 min at room temperature (RT) and then sonicated for 15 min
using an ultrasonic bath. The solution was then transferred into
a 250 mL Teon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave
was sealed and placed in a pre-heated oven at 180 (cid:3)C for 16 h
and subsequently allowed to cool down to RT naturally. The
obtained precipitate was centrifuged, washed several times with
distilled water and absolute ethanol, and dried at 60 (cid:3)C for 12 h
(sample A). Another set of experiments was also carried using
the same process with the exception of adjusting the pH to 5
and the hydrothermal treatment at 120 (cid:3)C for 16 h (sample B).
The resultant powders were stored in a desiccator.

Physicochemical characterization
Identication of the phases of the obtained samples was carried
out by X-ray diﬀraction analysis (XRD) (model Rigaku Miniex
X-ray diﬀractometer) with Cu Ka irradiation at l ¼ 1.5406 ˚A. The
surface morphology of the as-prepared samples was investi-
gated by eld emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM)
using a JEOL-JSM Model 6700F. Field emission transmission
electron microscopy (FETEM) images of the Sn3O4 samples was
performed on a JEOL, JEM-2200 FS transmission electron
microscope equipped with eld emission gun. Raman spectra
were recorded on a Jobin Yvon T64000 triple grating spec-
trometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen cooled charge coupled
device. Optical absorption spectra were generated using a JAS-
spectrophotometer. Room temperature photo-
COV-570
luminescence spectra (PL) of the aqueous suspensions were
recorded on an F-2500 uorescence spectrophotometer. For the
PL measurements, the water suspensions of the samples were
excited at a wavelength of 300 nm.

Photocatalytic study for the degradation of methylene blue
(MB) dye

Photocatalytic measurements of the synthesized product were
performed during the degradation of MB. P25 was used as
a standard material for comparison. All photocatalytic experi-
ments were carried out in a Pyrex beaker. 50 mg of catalyst was
added to 100 mL of a 10 ppm solution of MB and the mixture

A cylindrical quartz photochemical thermostatic reactor was

during the photo-reduction. 0.5 g of the as-prepared sample was
introduced into the reactor and irradiated with normal solar
light with constant stirring. The excess H2S was trapped in the
NaOH solution and the amount of hydrogen evolved was
measured using a graduated glass burette.

reaction mechanism, we can easily predict the probable growth
mechanism of the plate-like Sn3O4 nanostructure. The growth
of the plate-like nanostructure proceeds via nucleation of tiny
Sn3O4 nanoparticles and their subsequent evolution into
a layered plate shaped nanostructure. In the initial stage, the
nanoparticles self-align and exhibit one dimensional growth
due to the prolonged hydrothermal conditions. The growth is
faster at higher pH and hence the formation of layers of plates is
accelerated. Therefore, we observe a dense layered plate-like
structure of Sn3O4.

Generally, the photoluminescence (PL) spectra can provide
information on the separation and recombination of photo-
induced charge carriers. Fig. 7 shows the PL spectra of Sn3O4
and SnO2. The intensity of the PL spectrum is directly propor-
tional to the electron–hole recombination rate, i.e. the higher
the intensity of the spectrum, the higher the rate of electron–
hole recombination. In contrast, a lower intensity indicates that
more excited electrons are trapped and transferred stably
through the interface. The strong emission peaks observed at
334 nm and 363 nm indicate that defects exist in SnO2.
However, in the case of Sn3O4, the relative peak intensity was
decreased. It is well known that strong PL means a high
recombination of the photo-generated carriers suggesting low
photocatalytic activity. The decrease in the intensity of Sn3O4
indicates a minimization of the lattice defects when compared
to SnO2.

As understood from the optical measurements, the band gap
of Sn3O4 lies in the visible light region (2.76 eV) and that of SnO2
in the UV light region (3.21 eV). As discussed in the optical
properties section, the narrowing of the band gap of Sn3O4 was
responsible for the higher activity. This cras the band struc-
ture of Sn3O4 better for optical absorption under solar light as
well as charge carrier separation. As a result Sn3O4 shows
enhanced H2 production over SnO2. Further, the PL studies also
show the signicant inhibition of charge carrier recombination,
which was responsible for the higher photocatalytic activity.

4 Conclusions

To conclude, Sn3O4 and SnO2 nanostructures have been
synthesized via a facile hydrothermal method. pH plays an
important role in governing the phase of the synthesized tin
oxides. Sn3O4 was obtained at pH 13 while at pH 5 the obtained
product was SnO2. The sunlight driven photocatalytic activity
investigations demonstrate that Sn3O4 exhibits higher photo-
catalytic activity towards the degradation of MB as well as
hydrogen evolution, even better than P25 and SnO2. The supe-
rior photocatalytic activity of the as-synthesized Sn3O4 sample
can be attributed to its photo-absorption ability over a wide
spectral range due to the narrowing band gap as well as layered
nanoplates-like morphology.
