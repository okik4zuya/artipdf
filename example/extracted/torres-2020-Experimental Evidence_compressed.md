Abstract: Tin dioxide (SnO2) has intrinsic characteristics that do not 
favor its photocatalytic activity. However, we evidenced that surface 
modification  can  positively  influence  its  performance  for  CO2 
photoreduction  in  the  gas  phase.  The  hydroxylation  of  the  SnO2 
surface  played  a  role  in  the  CO2  affinity  decreasing  its  reduction 
potential.  The  results  showed  that  a  certain  selectivity  for  methane 
(CH4),  carbon  monoxide  (CO),  and  ethylene  (C2H4)  is  related  to 
different  SnO2  hydrothermal  annealing.  The  best  performance  was 
seen for SnO2 annealed at 150oC, with a production of 20.4 µmol g-1 
for CH4 and 16.45 µmol g-1 for CO, while for SnO2 at 200oC the system 
produced  more  C2H4,  probably  due  to  a  decrease  of  surface  -OH 
groups. 

Several  semiconductors,  highlighted  by  TiO2,  have  photocatalytic 
activity to promote redox reactions, such as the oxidation of organic 
substances  or  reduction  of  molecules  such  as  CO2  or  N2.  These 
reactions  occur  through  photoelectrochemical  processes,  driven  by 
the  charge  separation  during  illumination  and  specific  electronic 
structure of each material.[1] SnO2 has an electronic structure similar 
to TiO2, but its electronic mobility is almost 100 times higher, leading 
to high efficiency in the photogenerated electrons transference.[2] On 
the other hand, the position of its conduction band minimum (CBM) is 
located above the redox potential level of H+/H2 (-0.41 V vs. NHE, at 
pH  7),[3]  making  SnO2  limited  for  application  in  reduction  reactions. 
This feature also limits its application in photocatalytic oxidation since 
the reaction is controlled by the coupled reduction reaction. 

Electrochemical  experiments  indicate  that  SnO2  nanoparticles  are 
efficient  electrocatalysts  of  CO2  reduction  due  to  the  interaction  of 
carbonate  species  with  surface  Sn  hydroxides.[4]  In  this  case,  the 
energy for reduction is externally supplied, and analysis of reduction 
potential for SnO2 indicates that the oxide is not reduced to Sn in the 
necessary potential range for CO2 reduction, keeping stable. On the 
other  hand,  Sn  hydroxides  may  have  lower  reduction  potentials, 
favoring the redox process.[5] This surface interaction is expected to 
occur in photocatalytic systems since no bias is necessary to promote 
it, but the reaction would be limited by the band's energy.[6] Therefore, 
to overcome the limitation, the hydroxylation of SnO2 can, in principle, 
acts  as  a  charge  mediator,  reducing  the  reduction  potential  to 
favorable  ranges  for  CO2  reduction.[4,7]  Sn(OH)4,  the  most  probable 

hydroxide  in  sol-gel  synthesized  SnO2  surfaces,  is  expected  to  be 
reduced to Sn(OH)2 at lower potentials than H+/H2 and CO2/CO. Other 
hydroxides  can  be  formed  depending  on  the  synthesis  condition, 
which  can  be  modified  by  crystallization  routes  –  such  as 
hydrothermal annealing.[8]

SnO2  nanoparticles  were  synthesized  at  room  temperature  and 
annealed  via  hydrothermal  conditions  at  different  temperatures.  In 
detail,  the  SnO2  was  synthesized  as  follows  (Fig.  S1):  0.564  g  of 
SnCl2.2H2O  (Sigma-Aldrich)  was  dissolved  in  100  mL  of  ethanol 
anhydrous (99.5% purity, Dinâmica) and then under vigorous stirring, 
22.5 mL of distilled water was added dropwise at room temperature 
for 12 h.[9,10] After that, the suspension was cleaned by dialysis in 5 L 
of distilled water, often renewed, until removing all chloride ions, which 
was confirmed by tests with AgNO3 solution (0.1 mol L-1).[11] Then, the 
solution inside the dialysis bag was dried at 70°C for 12 h, ground with 
an agate mortar, and named “SnO2-cop”. After that, the as-prepared 
sample was treated hydrothermally up to 200°C for 4 h.

The morphology and size of the SnO2-cop particles were examined 
by  scanning  electron  microscopy  (SEM)  and  transmission  electron 
microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1a-b). SnO2-cop sample is precipitated in very 
massive agglomerates, consisting of large and faceted xerogel blocks, 
from 30 and 60μm (Fig. 1a). The xerogel structure is supported by the 
high  surface  area  (188  m2  g-1)  and  composed  of  small  crystalline 
particles (Figure 1b) of 4 nm, with adjacent lattice spacing (0.34 nm) 
referring to the planes (110).[12,13] Line defects (white arrows), typical 
of  oriented  attachment  growth,  are  seen  evidencing  the  growth 
mechanism. The X-ray diffraction patterns (Fig. 1c) indicate that the 
hydrothermal  annealing  did  not  affect  the  phase,  all  indexed  as 
tetragonal cassiterite phase (JCPDS No. 41-1445). The wide peaks 
and low intensity are characteristics of the amorphous nature of the 
samples.[14,15]  The  peak  sharpening  for  the  sample  at  200°C  is 
associated  with  the  oriented  growth  of  SnO2  the  particles,  as 
expected.[16] The bandgap value (Fig. S3) found for the commercial 
sample (3.7 eV) agrees with the literature, while to the other samples, 
the values were lower.[17,18] The bandgap values for the SnO2-cop and 
SnO2-200  are  3.2  and  3.5  eV,  respectively,  a  slight  blueshift  in  the 
bandgap energy.

A comparison of the total number of water groups at surface is done

the composition is the same). Therefore, the intensity of H2O and OH

water molecules, which are clearly seen by the quotient AH2O/ASn-O-Sn

bonded) for SnO2-cop is twice the observed for SnO2-200, indicating 
the  water  elimination.  These  data  also  reveal  that,  despite  the

seem to  be intermediary,  showing  almost the same  weakly  bonded

Finally,  SnO2-200  has  lower  content  of  both  weakly-bonded  and

These molecules are possibly regarded to the surface hydroxylation 
of SnO2, coordinated as Sn(OH)4, as a remnant of polycondensation 
step during coprecitation.[21] These OH groups undergo oxidation by 
the  holes  (h+)  formed  in the catalyst  valence  band  when  under  UV, 
leading to the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) with high oxidizing 
power (-SnOH + h+ ↔ -Sn2+ + •OH).[22] Adsorbed OH- groups on the 
catalyst  surface  this  potential  to  1.6V  vs  NHE,  favoring  the  radical 
formation.[3] Therefore, the attack of these radicals leads to oxidation 
reactions,  indicating  that  the  indirect  photodegradation  mechanism 
plays an important role in the photoactivity of the synthesized SnO2 
samples.[21,23] 

Fig. 2a indicates the potential of SnO2 samples to photoreduce CO2 
to C1 species. Although the intrinsic characteristics of SnO2 are not 
favorable to its photoactivity, these results reveal that the conditions 
used in the reactions were probably essential for selective response 
observed to certain  products. The  samples  were  selective for three 
different  products,  CH4,  CO,  and  C2H4,  respectively  (Fig.  2a),  with 
higher CH4 selectivity for all samples except for 200oC-annealed (Fig. 
2c).  Fig.  2b  shows  the  number  of  electrons  transferred  for  each 
product[24]  according  to  Equation  1  (see  in  Supporting  Information), 
knowing that the transference of 8e- are necessary for the production 
of  CH4 from  CO2  reduction,  2e- for  CO  and  12e-  for  C2H4.  The  gas-
phase  reactor  implies that  the  CO2 concentration  is  extremely  high, 
considering  that  humidified,  high  purity  CO2  is  purged  within  the 
system for 20 min. Thus, taking into account the acidic nature of CO2 
molecules,  its  adsorption  will  reduce  the  local  pH,  consequently 
lowering  the  potential  for  CO2  reduction.[25]  To  determine  the  acid-
base  nature  of  the  SnO2  surface,  the  isoelectric  point  of  sample 
suspensions at different pH values (Figure S6) was determined, which 
predicts  the  adsorption  behavior  of  CO2  molecules  during  the 
photoreduction process. As expected, although the values found were 
close  for  all  samples  and  in  accordance  with  the  literature,  a  small 
deviation  to  alkaline  behavior  is  seen  to  SnO2-100,  due  to  the 
reduction  of  crystalline  surface  water  (as  evidenced  by  FTIR 
measurements), which favors that this reaction can take place in lower 
local  pH  since  below  IEP  its  surface  is  positively  charged.[7,10]. 

However, in addition to CH4 and CO, we also observed the production 
of small amounts of C2H4 (Fig. 2c). An increase in C2H4 selectivity is 
seen  for  200oC-annealed  sample,  considering  the  total  catalytic 
activity.  We  hypothesize  that  the higher  annealing  temperature  has 
led  to  higher  growth  and  reduction  of  surface  -OH  groups,  which  is 
consistent with our results.[26] It is worth to mention that these results 
were obtained with pristine SnO2 and using a low-power lamp (18 W).

In conclusion, we demonstrated that SnO2 could be photocatalytically 
active for CO2 reduction in the solid-gas system (CO2-water gaseous 
mixture)  under  some  synthesis  conditions,  easily  modified  by 
hydrothermal  annealing.  Our  results  indicate  that  the  structural 
surface water (surface hydroxylation) plays a role in its photoactivity, 
and product selectivity can be tuned by modifying the nature of these 
surface hydroxides by crystallization. Moreover, the catalyst efficiency 
was  higher  than  previously  reported  papers,  including  compared  to 
TiO2. These results demonstrate that SnO2 should be more studied 
for CO2 reduction due to its advantages, as the high chemical stability, 
easy  synthesis  of  small  and  uniform  nanoparticles,  and  good 
electronic properties.
